# Most AI strategy is a procurement plan

A real AI strategy names three things: the jobs you will change, how you will know each change worked, and what you will stop doing to make room. Most documents called an AI strategy name none of them. They name tools. Which assistant every employee gets, which platform the data goes into, which vendor is "preferred." That is a procurement plan, and a procurement plan is not wrong, it's just not the thing you needed.

Here is a quick test. Take the document and delete every product name. What is left? If the answer is a page of adjectives like "bold" and "responsible," you were holding a shopping list.

A strategy is a set of choices that excludes something. "We will use AI across the business" excludes nothing, so it decides nothing. Compare it with "We will have an agent produce the first draft of every vendor invoice reconciliation by March, and we will stop paying two contractors to do it by hand." One of those you can fail at. That's how you know it's the strategy.

So start with jobs, not departments. Pick three. Not thirty, because thirty is how you end up with thirty pilots and no owner for any of them. A job is something with an input, an output and someone who currently does it: coding inbound tickets, chasing late purchase orders, preparing the monthly variance commentary. Smaller than a function, bigger than a task.

Then write down how you will know. This is the part that gets skipped, and it's the same discipline as [writing the test before you hire the agent](/blog/2026/10/12/write-the-test-before-you-hire-the-agent/). If you can't say what a correct result looks like, you can't measure whether the change worked, and the quarterly review becomes a vote on vibes.

Then the uncomfortable one: what stops. Every job you hand to an agent leaves a gap somewhere. A report nobody reads anymore. A meeting. A contractor. A queue of work people did only because the old process created it. If the strategy never removes anything, nothing was actually delegated. You added a tool and kept the work, which is the most expensive way to adopt anything.

Notice what's missing from all this: the model. I'd argue that's correct. Models change every few months and anyone can rent the same ones. The parts that last are your data, your process, and your decisions about who may do what. A strategy built on a specific vendor's roadmap expires when the roadmap does. One built on jobs, measures and stopping rules survives a model swap.

None of this means buying tools is silly. You will buy tools. But decide the jobs first and the tools become an easy question with a short answer, instead of a long one with a slide deck.
