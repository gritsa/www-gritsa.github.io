This morning I added a small text file to this website. It's called [llms.txt](/llms.txt), and as far as I can tell, no human being will ever read it on purpose.

That's fine. It was written for a different reader: the language models that now read websites on behalf of people who don't have time to. It tells them, in plain sentences, who Gritsa is, what we sell and where everything lives.

Most of the AI conversation in business points inward. Which jobs can agents take over? Which model? Build or buy? Good questions, all of them. But I think there's a more urgent one that gets far less airtime: what happens when the agent is sitting on the other side of the table?

It's already sitting there. In [G2's March 2026 survey](https://learn.g2.com/g2-2026-ai-search-insight-report) of 1,076 B2B decision makers, 51% said they start software research in an AI chatbot more often than in Google. And 69% said a chatbot had surfaced something that sent them to a different vendor than the one they expected. Your shortlist is increasingly written by something that has never seen your logo.

## What the agent actually sees

An assistant researching vendors doesn't watch your hero video. It isn't moved by your brand colors or the confident photo of your team. It reads, and it's hunting for answers to four plain questions.

What do you do? Who is it for? Roughly what does it cost? Why should anyone believe you?

If those answers sit in clear sentences on pages it can reach, you're in the running. If they're locked in a PDF, hidden behind a "contact us for pricing" button, or scattered across a homepage written to impress rather than inform, the agent does what a hurried human does. It moves on. The difference is that the human might have come back.

## What does being ready for AI buyers mean?

Being ready for AI buyers means a machine can find, understand and trust what you sell without a person translating for it. In practice that's pages that answer the questions buyers ask, facts that can be checked, prices or price ranges in writing, a sitemap so crawlers find everything, and a short summary file that tells a model where to look.

None of that is new. It's the work good marketers always meant to get around to. It stayed optional because a determined human would dig for the answer. Agents don't dig. There's always another vendor one query away.

## Reading is only the first step

Today most of these agents read. More of them will soon act. Last year Stripe and OpenAI released the [Agentic Commerce Protocol](https://stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-openai-instant-checkout), an open standard that lets an assistant complete a purchase inside the chat. The Model Context Protocol already gives any agent a standard way to call into a company's systems: check stock, request a quote, book a meeting.

Follow that line and a company's MCP server starts to look like its storefront for machines. Not a chatbot bolted onto the homepage. A real interface, with rules about what an outside agent may see and do, sitting on data that is accurate at the moment it's asked. For a B2B services firm it will start small. An agent that can pull your case studies, check when a team is free, ask for a ballpark. I suspect that's closer than most roadmaps assume.

## The same work pays twice

Here's the part I find most interesting. The work that makes a company legible to outside agents is almost identical to the work that lets it use agents inside. Current, trustworthy data, which is why I'd argue most agent projects are [data engineering](/services/data-engineering/) projects underneath. Processes written down well enough that a machine could follow them. Clear decisions about who, or what, is allowed to do which things.

Do that work once and it pays you twice. Skip it, and you end up with internal AI tools that never quite work, and a website the outside agents can't make sense of. Same root cause, two bills.

So try something today. Ask two or three assistants what your company does, who it's for and what it costs. Read the answers the way a buyer would. If they're vague or wrong, the model is rarely the problem. The problem is what you've given it to read, and fixing that is the first step in any [AI agent project](/services/ai-agents/) worth doing.

Your next customer might be an agent. Give it something worth reading.
